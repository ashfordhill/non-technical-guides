# Choosing Books Wisely

There are millions of books in the world. A human would need thousands of lifespans to have the time to complete them all. Knowing this, it's important to be selective with book choices.

I was revisiting a book I read a couple years ago called *Why We Sleep* by Matthew Walker. Matthew Walker is a neuroscientist. Many people might consider him a figure of authority on brain-related topics. However, even authors with impressive credentials can sometimes sensationalize information in order to drive their points home. We'll revisit Walker's work later on.

Beyond stretching the truth (or even just making shit up), scientific nonfiction books can also age poorly; something that was certain 100 years ago might be untrue today.

When it comes to non-scientific nonfiction, I find that the age of the book really doesn't matter so much. In this domain, when information from 2 or more different sources share a common observation, it stands out to me as some possible truth. This can occur in religious and philosophical texts. In these, there tend to be overlapping themes for topics like purpose, love, well-being, etc. 

In the self-help book area we often see regurgitated and repackaged information from both scientific and non-scientific nonfiction:

  -  *7 Ways to Silence Your Inner Critic*
  -  *The Secret to Living a Successful Life*
  -  *How to Attract Love in Your Life and Keep It*

These pop-psychology books have their value in that they can help dense texts become palatable to the general public. However, liberties are sometimes taken when it comes to their "science-backed" claims.

Book choice is important. If a book's reviews are positive and was written by Dr. Big Brain from Top University, it doesn't mean they're a trustworthy author.

## Back to Sleep

Coming back to *Why We Sleep* having scientific inaccuracies, this was brought to my attention after reading [a post](https://guzey.com/books/why-we-sleep/) written by Alexey Guzey, some intellectual blogger. He also brings to light some intriguing information about Walker's claimed credentials.

Guzey debunks claims that I have fallen for like scaremongering around lifespan and cancer occurences being drastically higher for people who get less than 6 hours (or whatever) of sleep. This is not actually supported by current evidence. We probably don't have enough quality data to draw many strong conclusions at this point anyway. History has many examples of successful, brilliant short- and long- sleepers, nap-takers and insomniacs alike. Everyone's body works differently. 

While there is still compelling and [currently] accurate information in the book, it's the sensationalist, extreme claims that Walker made that have been imprinted in my memory. Maybe that's intentional to ultimately drive sales, who knows. In any case, going forward I can't really recommend *Why We Sleep* to anyone.
