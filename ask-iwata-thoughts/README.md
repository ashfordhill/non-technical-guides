# Ask Iwata Thoughts

Nice bits around *Ask Iwata*. Iwata Satoru was the president of Nintendo for over a decade in the early 2000's until his untimely death in 2015.

## Early Challenges

In his early-ish career, Iwata became the president of HAL Laboratory, a game studio that still collaborates with Nintendo today. At the time, HAL was in 1.5 billion yen ($9.5m) in debt. 

>"I spent my first month as president interviewing everybody at the company. The discoveries were endles. [..] The idea was to figure out everybody's strengths and weaknessses."

Decades later, he is able to calmly recall these details. But make no mistake - it was a very stressful period for him.

>"In retrospect, this difficult period was an incredible learning experience for me."


## Humans Shine in Collaboration

> "How do you know when a project is going well? When someone points to a gray area in the initial plan, then asks you "Hey, can I take care of this?" and follows through. Projects where this keeps on happening tend to end up going well. On the other hand, when this phenomenon fails to occur, you might finish up all right, but things will feel a bit dyfunctional, which is never a good sign."

Throughout the book, themes of collaboration and people with different strengths coming together seem common in the path towards successful outcomes. 

>"No job is ever done alone. Someone else will always play a role. Companies provide a structure that allows for a variety of individuals to pool their efforts and accomplish huge tasks that could not be done alone."

## Feedback Loops

Iwata describes Shigeru Miyamoto as someone he admires; Miyamoto is a reknown game designer behind titles like The Legend of Zelda, Mario, Donkey Kong and more. 

Miyamoto would take his game prototypes to random employees in the company and sit behind them as they played for the first time, silently observing how they interacted. This feedback was important to see how a fresh set of eyes viewed his design. 

> "A person who makes games can't sit down with everyone who buys them and explain, 'Here's how I designed things. Here's how to play.' Obviously that would be impossible. The product needs to be self-explanatory."
