> "Do what you love and you'll never work a day in your life!"

I hate this saying.

## Catholics

> "We were created with a vocation to work. The goal should not be that technological progress increasingly replace human work, for this would be detrimental to humanity. Work is a necessity, part of the meaning of life on this earth, a path to growth, human development and personal fulfillment. Helping the poor financially must always be a provisional solution in the face of pressing needs. The broader objective should always be to allow them a dignified life through work."
>
> — *Pope Francis*, [Laudato Si](https://www.vatican.va/content/francesco/en/encyclicals/documents/papa-francesco_20150524_enciclica-laudato-si.html)


I don't consider myself religious, but I believe there's useful wisdom that can be harvested across all religions. It's the passing down of general knowledge about the human condition over generations that is valuable.

Pope Francis has said that work is necessary for human dignity. Without work, what are we? Endless consumers. Endless consumption is unhealthy, if for no other reason than endless *anything* is unhealthy.

## The Future of my Work

I find the best parts of working aren't when I get to "do what I love", but when I'm helpful to others by doing something I don't love.

When I'm able to make someone's day flow more freely and unblock them by doing undesireable DevOps work, dealing with IT issues, or passing on tribal knowledge and tricks, I feel purposeful because I've helped someone.

I especially want new engineers to get the same wonderful learning opportunities I was fortunate to have in my early career, perhaps from some subconscious desire to create a bit of fairness in an unfair world. I want to be a good role model who has a positive impact on others, however small or transient that impact may be. 

I'm getting older. My back hurts. My mind wanders. Grinding it out in an IDE several hours a day, 5 days a week, trying to hyperfocus - it isn't a particular source of joy for me.

I'm realizing why many SWEs don't want to do pure code for decades and decades. It's not because one becomes less sharp - it's just that priorities shift. I want my finite life to have meaning. Often times, "meaning" involves interpersonal relationships.

## Work and Happiness

When people are unhappy at work, the root issue isn't necessarily the tasks they are doing:

  - Maybe they feel taken for granted. 

  - Maybe they feel frustration towards their coworkers.

  - Maybe they're jealous they don't get to work with a favorite colleague, while someone else does.

I've had a love/hate relationship with my job for many years. When I'm in my best spirits, I realize that the issue isn't the job. I certainly have a lot to be thankful for. The issue is my perception.

It's easy to take the beautiful things in our life for granted. We all do it; it's human nature. We chase chase chase. The goalposts always move, we compare our lives to others, often perceiving other people as "having it all". We get caught up in corporate/consumer/social games that don't really mean anything in the end. This type of behavior certainly doesn't lead to inner peace - quite the opposite!

## Buddhists

In Buddhism, it's generally said that we are the cause of our own suffering. I think this is true. Humans are the only species that suffer due to our own thoughts. Our intelligence is a double-edged sword.

Here are some of my favorite quotes from Thich Naht Hanh's *No Mud, No Lotus*:

>“The soil of our mind contains many seeds, positive and negative. We are the gardeners who identify, water, and cultivate the best seeds.”

>"The art of happiness is also the art of suffering well."

>"Suffering has its beneficial aspects. It can be an excellent teacher."
